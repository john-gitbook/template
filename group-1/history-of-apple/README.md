# History of Apple

## History of Apple

Apple grew from a small personal-computer company into one of the world's largest technology businesses. Its history includes breakthrough products, difficult transitions, design-led reinvention, and recurring debates about control, privacy, labor, and competition.

The company was founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak, and Ronald Wayne. Its early success came from making computers approachable for homes, schools, and small businesses. Later, Apple changed the music, phone, tablet, and wearable markets.

### Origins and founding

In the mid-1970s, computers were usually expensive machines used by large organizations. Hobbyists began building smaller systems from kits and sharing ideas through clubs. The Homebrew Computer Club in Silicon Valley was one important meeting place for this community.

Steve Wozniak designed a compact computer around the MOS Technology 6502 processor. His design prioritized clever engineering and low cost. Steve Jobs recognized that the machine could be sold as a finished product rather than only as a hobbyist project.

Jobs, Wozniak, and Ronald Wayne formed Apple Computer Company on April 1, 1976. Wayne drafted the first partnership agreement and created an early logo. He left the company less than two weeks later, selling his ownership back to the other founders.

The first product, the Apple I, was largely sold as an assembled circuit board. Buyers supplied their own case, keyboard, display, and power supply. The Byte Shop, a computer retailer in Mountain View, ordered fifty units, helping establish Apple as a business rather than an informal project.

### The Apple II era

The Apple II launched in 1977 and became Apple’s first major commercial success. Unlike many hobbyist machines, it arrived as a consumer-ready product. It had a plastic enclosure, a built-in keyboard, color graphics, expansion slots, and support for cassette storage.

The system evolved through several models. Its open architecture allowed third-party companies to create hardware and software. That flexibility helped the Apple II become common in schools, homes, and offices.

VisiCalc, released in 1979, made the Apple II especially useful for business. It was an early spreadsheet application. Small-business owners could model budgets, forecasts, and financial plans on a personal computer. VisiCalc became a strong reason to buy the hardware.

Apple’s sales expanded quickly. The company went public in December 1980. The initial public offering created substantial wealth for many employees and investors. It also gave Apple funds and visibility as personal computing became a larger industry.

### Lisa, Xerox PARC, and graphical computing

Apple explored new computing interfaces during the late 1970s and early 1980s. Jobs and other Apple employees visited Xerox PARC, where researchers had developed a graphical user interface, windows, icons, menus, a mouse, and Ethernet networking.

Apple did not invent each of these concepts. It adapted them into commercial products for a broader audience. The work influenced both the Lisa and Macintosh projects.

The Apple Lisa arrived in 1983. It used a graphical interface and mouse, but its price was far beyond most buyers’ budgets. The Lisa demonstrated Apple’s belief that future computers would be visual and easier to use. Commercially, it did not meet expectations.

The Macintosh project pursued a similar vision with lower cost and a smaller team. The project developed a focused computer experience. Its hardware and software were tightly integrated, a pattern that later became central to Apple’s identity.

### The Macintosh launches

Apple introduced the original Macintosh in January 1984. Its famous “1984” television commercial presented the product during the Super Bowl. The campaign framed the Macintosh as an alternative to conformity in computing.

The original Macintosh had a compact all-in-one design, a nine-inch display, a mouse, and a graphical interface. It shipped with 128 kilobytes of memory. That constraint made early software development difficult, but the machine made graphical computing visible to many people.

Desktop publishing gave the Macintosh a durable market. In 1985, Apple released the LaserWriter printer. Aldus PageMaker provided page-layout software, while Adobe PostScript enabled high-quality typography. Together, the Macintosh, LaserWriter, and PageMaker changed publishing workflows for designers and small studios.

The Macintosh also established Apple’s preference for a controlled platform. Apple designed the hardware, operating system, core interface, and much of the user experience. This could limit choice, but it created a more consistent system than many competing PCs.

### Leadership conflict and Jobs’s departure

Apple recruited John Sculley from PepsiCo as chief executive in 1983. Jobs reportedly asked him whether he wanted to sell sugared water or change the world. The partnership initially gave Apple a more experienced executive leader.

Tension grew as Macintosh sales and organizational priorities changed. In 1985, Apple’s board supported Sculley in a dispute over control of the Macintosh division. Jobs lost day-to-day authority and resigned later that year.

Jobs founded NeXT after leaving Apple. NeXT built advanced workstations for higher education and business. The computers sold in limited volume, but NeXT developed software that would later become important to Apple. Tim Berners-Lee created the first World Wide Web server and browser on a NeXT computer.

Wozniak had stepped back from full-time work at Apple earlier in the decade. He remained associated with the company as a co-founder, but pursued other interests and projects.

### Apple in the late 1980s

During the late 1980s, Apple expanded the Macintosh line and built a strong business around education, publishing, and creative work. The company released desktop and portable Macs with increasingly capable processors and displays.

Apple also invested in software and media technologies. HyperCard, introduced in 1987, let people create linked stacks of information, buttons, and media. It influenced later ideas about hypertext and interactive authoring.

The company entered several new product categories. Some products succeeded, while others did not. Apple’s approach often involved introducing new interaction models before the market was fully ready.

The Macintosh operating system remained easy to use, but its technical foundations aged. As software grew more complex, Apple struggled to add modern features such as protected memory and preemptive multitasking without breaking compatibility.

### The 1990s: growth, competition, and complexity

The personal-computer market changed rapidly in the 1990s. Microsoft Windows gained share on IBM-compatible PCs. Intel processors became the dominant standard for many desktop computers. Microsoft’s software platform benefitted from broad hardware support and a large developer ecosystem.

Apple continued to sell Macs, but its market share declined. The company had higher prices than many PC manufacturers and faced pressure from lower-cost Windows machines. Apple also created a confusing number of product models, each with different features and names.

Apple experimented with licensing the Macintosh operating system to outside computer makers. Companies such as Power Computing produced Mac-compatible systems. The program increased the number of Mac systems, but it also risked shifting sales away from Apple’s own higher-margin hardware.

The company pursued several ambitious technologies. QuickTime advanced digital video on personal computers. The Newton MessagePad introduced handwriting recognition and a handheld computing concept. Newton drew attention but struggled with cost, performance, and recognition accuracy.

In 1993, Sculley left Apple. Michael Spindler became chief executive, followed by Gil Amelio in 1996. Each leader faced pressure to simplify the product line, improve the operating system, and restore profitability.

### The operating-system crisis

Apple’s aging System software created a strategic problem. The company worked on a successor called Copland, but the project became delayed and difficult to complete. Apple considered several paths to acquire or license a modern operating system.

In late 1996, Apple announced it would buy NeXT for approximately $429 million. The acquisition brought NeXTSTEP technology, experienced engineers, and Steve Jobs back to Apple as an advisor.

NeXT’s software used a Unix-based foundation and object-oriented development tools. It became the basis for Apple’s future operating systems. The decision allowed Apple to abandon the delayed Copland effort and begin a clearer technical transition.

Gil Amelio left Apple in 1997. Jobs became interim chief executive later that year. He would eventually drop the “interim” label, but it captured the uncertainty around Apple’s future at the time.

### Apple’s return under Steve Jobs

Jobs simplified Apple’s product strategy. A famous product grid divided the business into consumer and professional customers, with desktop and portable computers for each group. This reduced a sprawling catalog to a few clearer priorities.

The first major result was the iMac, introduced in 1998. The iMac used a colorful translucent enclosure, removed the floppy-disk drive, and adopted USB ports. It made a desktop computer look distinct from beige office hardware.

The iMac helped revive Apple’s brand. It also showed how product design could become a business advantage. Apple treated the computer as a complete object, combining industrial design, software, packaging, advertising, and retail presentation.

Apple introduced the Power Mac G3 and PowerBook G3 during this period. It also ended the Mac clone licensing program. Apple returned to selling a more tightly controlled hardware and software experience.

In 2001, Apple released Mac OS X. The system combined the familiar Mac interface with the Unix-based architecture derived from NeXT. Its Aqua interface introduced translucent visual elements, while the underlying Darwin system brought stability and modern networking foundations.

### Music, the iPod, and iTunes

Apple began reshaping digital music in 2001. The first iPod was a portable music player with a small hard drive and a scroll wheel. Its “1,000 songs in your pocket” message made its benefit easy to understand.

Early iPods worked primarily with Macs. That changed when Apple released a Windows-compatible iPod and iTunes software. The broader support helped the iPod become a mainstream consumer product.

The iTunes Store launched in 2003. It sold individual songs through a simple online storefront. Apple negotiated with record labels at a time when unauthorized file sharing had disrupted the music industry.

The iPod, iTunes, and iTunes Store formed an integrated system. Hardware, desktop software, online purchasing, and synchronization worked together. This model became a template for Apple’s later device-and-services ecosystem.

By the middle of the decade, the iPod dominated portable digital music. Apple introduced smaller iPod mini, nano, and shuffle models. The iPod also became a cultural symbol, supported by silhouettes advertising and white earbuds.

### Apple retail stores

Apple opened its first retail stores in 2001. Many observers questioned whether a computer company could operate successful stores. Jobs believed Apple needed direct control over how customers encountered its products.

The stores emphasized open product displays, trained staff, technical support through the Genius Bar, and locations in shopping centers and cities. Apple Stores became important sales channels and physical expressions of the company’s design standards.

Retail gave Apple a direct relationship with customers. It reduced dependence on third-party retailers and gave the company a place to demonstrate new products. The stores later supported device setup, repairs, education, and trade-in programs.

### The iPhone changes the company

Apple announced the iPhone in January 2007 and released it in June. Jobs described it as an iPod, a phone, and an internet communicator. The device replaced the physical keyboard common on smartphones with a multi-touch display.

The iPhone built on technologies Apple had developed for the iPod, Mac OS X, and its hardware design work. It offered a mobile browser, visual voicemail, a camera, media playback, and a new touch-first interface.

The first iPhone lacked a third-party app platform. In 2008, Apple released the App Store alongside iPhone OS 2.0. Developers could sell applications directly to users, and Apple controlled distribution, review, payments, and platform rules.

The App Store created a large mobile software economy. It also made Apple’s platform governance more consequential. Developers relied on Apple for access to iPhone users, while Apple set technical requirements and content policies.

Apple renamed iPhone OS to iOS in 2010. The iPhone became the company’s most important product line, generating substantial revenue and influencing competitors across the mobile industry.

### The iPad and post-PC computing

Apple introduced the iPad in 2010. The product sat between a smartphone and a laptop. Some critics saw it as an oversized iPhone, but the iPad created a major tablet market and found uses in homes, schools, healthcare, aviation, and retail.

The iPad benefited from the App Store and iOS developer ecosystem. Its larger screen supported reading, video, games, drawing, and specialized applications. Apple later added keyboards, stylus support, multitasking features, and its own iPadOS platform.

The term “post-PC” described a shift toward mobile devices for common tasks. Apple did not argue that traditional computers would disappear. Instead, it positioned phones and tablets as simpler tools for many activities that once required a desktop or laptop.

### Leadership transition to Tim Cook

Steve Jobs took medical leaves during his final years at Apple. Tim Cook, Apple’s chief operating officer, managed many day-to-day operations during those periods. Cook had joined Apple in 1998 and played a central role in supply-chain management.

Jobs resigned as chief executive in August 2011 and died in October of that year. Cook became chief executive. The transition raised questions about whether Apple could sustain its pace of product development without its co-founder.

Under Cook, Apple expanded several existing product lines and introduced new categories. The company also became larger in revenue, market value, and global reach. Its leadership style became more operational and collaborative than the highly personalized public image associated with Jobs.

### Services and the ecosystem

As iPhone sales matured, Apple placed greater emphasis on services. These include the App Store, iCloud, Apple Music, Apple TV+, Apple Arcade, Apple Pay, Fitness+, and subscription bundles.

Services deepen the value of Apple devices and create recurring revenue. They also tie products together. A customer may use an iPhone, Mac, iPad, Apple Watch, AirPods, cloud storage, media subscriptions, and payment services within one account.

This integration is central to Apple’s strategy. Devices are designed to work together through features such as AirDrop, Handoff, Continuity, Family Sharing, and iCloud synchronization. The approach can make switching away from Apple less convenient, which attracts both praise and regulatory scrutiny.

### Apple Watch, AirPods, and wearables

Apple announced the Apple Watch in 2014 and released it in 2015. It began as a flexible wearable device with messaging, fitness, notifications, and apps. Over time, Apple increasingly focused the product on health, safety, and activity tracking.

Features such as heart-rate notifications, fall detection, emergency calling, electrocardiogram support in some regions, and sleep tracking broadened the watch’s role. Health features also required careful regulatory and clinical considerations.

AirPods launched in 2016. Their wireless design and easy pairing helped make true wireless earbuds a major product category. Apple later expanded the line with AirPods Pro and AirPods Max.

Wearables, home products, and accessories became a significant part of Apple’s business. These devices also reinforced the broader ecosystem around the iPhone.

### Apple Silicon and the Mac transition

Apple announced its transition from Intel processors to Apple-designed chips for Macs in 2020. The company had already designed processors for iPhone and iPad devices for years. Bringing that work to the Mac gave Apple more control over performance, power efficiency, and product schedules.

The first Macs with the M1 chip arrived in late 2020. Reviews often highlighted strong performance and long battery life. Apple used translation technology called Rosetta 2 to help many existing Intel-based applications run during the transition.

Apple Silicon continued with later M-series chips across laptops, desktops, and professional machines. The transition marked another major architecture shift for the Mac. Earlier transitions included moves from Motorola 68k to PowerPC, PowerPC to Intel, and the adoption of Mac OS X.

The change strengthened Apple’s vertical integration. The company now designs key processors, operating systems, many applications, and the devices that run them.

### Privacy and security

Apple increasingly presents privacy as a product principle. The company has added controls for app tracking, permissions, encrypted communications, and on-device processing. It argues that personal data should not become a company’s primary business model.

In 2021, App Tracking Transparency required iPhone and iPad apps to request permission before tracking users across other companies’ apps and websites. The policy affected advertising businesses that relied on cross-app data collection.

Apple’s privacy claims also attract scrutiny. Critics examine how Apple’s own services use data, how platform rules affect competitors, and how privacy features operate in practice. Privacy is both a technical design question and a policy choice.

Security remains important across Apple’s platforms. Apple uses hardware-backed security features, frequent software updates, code signing, app review, and encryption. No platform is immune from vulnerabilities, so these systems require ongoing maintenance.

### App Store rules and antitrust questions

Apple’s control over the App Store has become a major policy issue. Developers have challenged the company’s commission structure, payment rules, review process, and restrictions on directing users to other payment options.

Governments and regulators in several regions have investigated Apple’s platform practices. The European Union’s Digital Markets Act and other laws have pushed Apple to make changes in some markets. The details vary by jurisdiction and continue to evolve.

Apple argues that its rules protect users from fraud, malware, and privacy abuse. Critics argue that the company uses control over iOS distribution to advantage its own services and collect fees from competitors. The debate reflects a broader question: how much control should a platform owner have over a computing ecosystem?

### Manufacturing and supply chains

Apple designs many products in California but relies on a global manufacturing network. Its suppliers and assembly partners operate across several countries, especially in Asia. This system lets Apple produce devices at large scale.

The company’s supply chain has been a major competitive strength. Apple coordinates components, manufacturing capacity, logistics, and retail distribution with unusual precision. Tim Cook’s operations experience helped build this capability.

The scale also creates responsibility and risk. Apple has faced criticism and reporting about working conditions, labor rights, environmental impacts, mineral sourcing, and dependence on particular regions. The company publishes supplier-responsibility reports and conducts audits, but outside groups continue to assess its progress.

Apple has also worked to diversify parts of its manufacturing footprint. Geopolitical tensions, pandemic disruptions, and component shortages demonstrated the limits of concentrated supply chains.

### Environmental commitments

Apple has set goals related to carbon emissions, renewable energy, recycled materials, packaging, and product longevity. It says its corporate operations are carbon neutral and has stated a goal to make its entire product footprint carbon neutral by 2030.

Product design creates trade-offs. Smaller devices can use fewer materials and less energy, but tightly integrated designs can make independent repairs more difficult. Apple has expanded repair programs and provided parts and manuals for some products, while repair advocates continue to seek broader access.

The company has removed chargers from some product boxes, arguing that many customers already own compatible chargers. Supporters cite lower material use and shipping emissions. Critics question whether the change shifts costs to customers or complicates reuse.

### Apple in entertainment and spatial computing

Apple expanded its entertainment business through Apple TV+, launched in 2019. The service commissions and distributes original films and television programs. It competes with established streaming companies while remaining part of Apple’s broader services portfolio.

In 2024, Apple began selling Apple Vision Pro, a spatial-computing headset. The device combines high-resolution displays, cameras, sensors, hand and eye tracking, and a new interface called visionOS. It reflects Apple’s long-running interest in new ways to interact with digital content.

Vision Pro entered a market with previous virtual- and mixed-reality products. Its early audience focused on developers, professionals, and enthusiasts because of its price and first-generation status. Its longer-term importance remains uncertain.

### A company shaped by transitions

Apple’s history is defined by repeated transitions. It moved from hobbyist computers to consumer electronics. It moved from the Apple II to the Macintosh, from classic Mac software to Mac OS X, and from Intel-based Macs to Apple Silicon.

The company’s most successful periods followed clear product choices. The Apple II made personal computing practical. The Macintosh made graphical computing accessible. The iPod organized digital music. The iPhone put powerful computing in a pocket.

Apple also experienced failures and missed expectations. Lisa was too expensive. Newton arrived before its technology was ready. The 1990s product strategy became too complex. Those episodes shaped later decisions to focus on fewer products and integrate software with hardware.

Today, Apple remains a technology manufacturer, software company, media provider, retail operator, and platform owner. Its influence reaches far beyond the products it sells. The choices Apple makes about interfaces, privacy, distribution, materials, and standards affect customers, developers, suppliers, and competitors worldwide.

### Apple in the late 1980s

### The 1990s: growth, competition, and complexity

The personal-computer market changed rapidly in the 1990s. Microsoft Windows gained share on IBM-compatible PCs. Intel processors became the dominant standard for many desktop computers. Microsoft’s software platform benefitted from broad hardware support and a large developer ecosystem.

Apple continued to sell Macs, but its market share declined. The company had higher prices than many PC manufacturers and faced pressure from lower-cost Windows machines. Apple also created a confusing number of product models, each with different features and names.

### The operating-system crisis

Apple’s aging System software created a strategic problem. The company worked on a successor called Copland, but the project became delayed and difficult to complete. Apple considered several paths to acquire or license a modern operating system.

In late 1996, Apple announced it would buy NeXT for approximately $429 million. The acquisition brought NeXTSTEP technology, experienced engineers, and Steve Jobs back to Apple as an advisor.

### Apple’s return under Steve Jobs

Jobs simplified Apple’s product strategy. A famous product grid divided the business into consumer and professional customers, with desktop and portable computers for each group. This reduced a sprawling catalog to a few clearer priorities.

The first major result was the iMac, introduced in 1998. The iMac used a colorful translucent enclosure, removed the floppy-disk drive, and adopted USB ports. It made a desktop computer look distinct from beige office hardware.

Apple introduced the Power Mac G3 and PowerBook G3 during this period. It also ended the Mac clone licensing program. Apple returned to selling a more tightly controlled hardware and software experience.

### Music, the iPod, and iTunes

Apple began reshaping digital music in 2001. The first iPod was a portable music player with a small hard drive and a scroll wheel. Its “1,000 songs in your pocket” message made its benefit easy to understand.

Early iPods worked primarily with Macs. That changed when Apple released a Windows-compatible iPod and iTunes software. The broader support helped the iPod become a mainstream consumer product.

The iTunes Store launched in 2003. It sold individual songs through a simple online storefront. Apple negotiated with record labels at a time when unauthorized file sharing had disrupted the music industry.

### Apple retail stores

The stores emphasized open product displays, trained staff, technical support through the Genius Bar, and locations in shopping centers and cities. Apple Stores became important sales channels and physical expressions of the company’s design standards.

Retail gave Apple a direct relationship with customers. It reduced dependence on third-party retailers and gave the company a place to demonstrate new products. The stores later supported device setup, repairs, education, and trade-in programs.

### The iPhone changes the company

Apple announced the iPhone in January 2007 and released it in June. Jobs described it as an iPod, a phone, and an internet communicator. The device replaced the physical keyboard common on smartphones with a multi-touch display.

The iPhone built on technologies Apple had developed for the iPod, Mac OS X, and its hardware design work. It offered a mobile browser, visual voicemail, a camera, media playback, and a new touch-first interface.

The first iPhone lacked a third-party app platform. In 2008, Apple released the App Store alongside iPhone OS 2.0. Developers could sell applications directly to users, and Apple controlled distribution, review, payments, and platform rules.

The App Store created a large mobile software economy. It also made Apple’s platform governance more consequential. Developers relied on Apple for access to iPhone users, while Apple set technical requirements and content policies.

Apple renamed iPhone OS to iOS in 2010. The iPhone became the company’s most important product line, generating substantial revenue and influencing competitors across the mobile industry.

### The iPad and post-PC computing

Apple introduced the iPad in 2010. The product sat between a smartphone and a laptop. Some critics saw it as an oversized iPhone, but the iPad created a major tablet market and found uses in homes, schools, healthcare, aviation, and retail.

The iPad benefited from the App Store and iOS developer ecosystem. Its larger screen supported reading, video, games, drawing, and specialized applications. Apple later added keyboards, stylus support, multitasking features, and its own iPadOS platform.

The term “post-PC” described a shift toward mobile devices for common tasks. Apple did not argue that traditional computers would disappear. Instead, it positioned phones and tablets as simpler tools for many activities that once required a desktop or laptop.

### Leadership transition to Tim Cook

Steve Jobs took medical leaves during his final years at Apple. Tim Cook, Apple’s chief operating officer, managed many day-to-day operations during those periods. Cook had joined Apple in 1998 and played a central role in supply-chain management.

Jobs resigned as chief executive in August 2011 and died in October of that year. Cook became chief executive. The transition raised questions about whether Apple could sustain its pace of product development without its co-founder.

Under Cook, Apple expanded several existing product lines and introduced new categories. The company also became larger in revenue, market value, and global reach. Its leadership style became more operational and collaborative than the highly personalized public image associated with Jobs.

### Services and the ecosystem

Services deepen the value of Apple devices and create recurring revenue. They also tie products together. A customer may use an iPhone, Mac, iPad, Apple Watch, AirPods, cloud storage, media subscriptions, and payment services within one account.

This integration is central to Apple’s strategy. Devices are designed to work together through features such as AirDrop, Handoff, Continuity, Family Sharing, and iCloud synchronization. The approach can make switching away from Apple less convenient, which attracts both praise and regulatory scrutiny.

### Apple Watch, AirPods, and wearables

Wearables, home products, and accessories became a significant part of Apple’s business. These devices also reinforced the broader ecosystem around the iPhone.

## History of Apple

Apple grew from a small personal-computer company into one of the world's largest technology businesses. Its history includes breakthrough products, difficult transitions, design-led reinvention, and recurring debates about control, privacy, labor, and competition.

The company was founded in 1976 by Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak, and Ronald Wayne. Its early success came from making computers approachable for homes, schools, and small businesses. Later, Apple changed the music, phone, tablet, and wearable markets.

### Origins and founding

In the mid-1970s, computers were usually expensive machines used by large organizations. Hobbyists began building smaller systems from kits and sharing ideas through clubs. The Homebrew Computer Club in Silicon Valley was one important meeting place for this community.

Steve Wozniak designed a compact computer around the MOS Technology 6502 processor. His design prioritized clever engineering and low cost. Steve Jobs recognized that the machine could be sold as a finished product rather than only as a hobbyist project.

Jobs, Wozniak, and Ronald Wayne formed Apple Computer Company on April 1, 1976. Wayne drafted the first partnership agreement and created an early logo. He left the company less than two weeks later, selling his ownership back to the other founders.

The first product, the Apple I, was largely sold as an assembled circuit board. Buyers supplied their own case, keyboard, display, and power supply. The Byte Shop, a computer retailer in Mountain View, ordered fifty units, helping establish Apple as a business rather than an informal project.

### The Apple II era

The Apple II launched in 1977 and became Apple’s first major commercial success. Unlike many hobbyist machines, it arrived as a consumer-ready product. It had a plastic enclosure, a built-in keyboard, color graphics, expansion slots, and support for cassette storage.

The system evolved through several models. Its open architecture allowed third-party companies to create hardware and software. That flexibility helped the Apple II become common in schools, homes, and offices.

VisiCalc, released in 1979, made the Apple II especially useful for business. It was an early spreadsheet application. Small-business owners could model budgets, forecasts, and financial plans on a personal computer. VisiCalc became a strong reason to buy the hardware.

Apple’s sales expanded quickly. The company went public in December 1980. The initial public offering created substantial wealth for many employees and investors. It also gave Apple funds and visibility as personal computing became a larger industry.

### Lisa, Xerox PARC, and graphical computing

Apple explored new computing interfaces during the late 1970s and early 1980s. Jobs and other Apple employees visited Xerox PARC, where researchers had developed a graphical user interface, windows, icons, menus, a mouse, and Ethernet networking.

Apple did not invent each of these concepts. It adapted them into commercial products for a broader audience. The work influenced both the Lisa and Macintosh projects.

The Apple Lisa arrived in 1983. It used a graphical interface and mouse, but its price was far beyond most buyers’ budgets. The Lisa demonstrated Apple’s belief that future computers would be visual and easier to use. Commercially, it did not meet expectations.

The Macintosh project pursued a similar vision with lower cost and a smaller team. The project developed a focused computer experience. Its hardware and software were tightly integrated, a pattern that later became central to Apple’s identity.

### The Macintosh launches

Apple introduced the original Macintosh in January 1984. Its famous “1984” television commercial presented the product during the Super Bowl. The campaign framed the Macintosh as an alternative to conformity in computing.

The original Macintosh had a compact all-in-one design, a nine-inch display, a mouse, and a graphical interface. It shipped with 128 kilobytes of memory. That constraint made early software development difficult, but the machine made graphical computing visible to many people.

Desktop publishing gave the Macintosh a durable market. In 1985, Apple released the LaserWriter printer. Aldus PageMaker provided page-layout software, while Adobe PostScript enabled high-quality typography. Together, the Macintosh, LaserWriter, and PageMaker changed publishing workflows for designers and small studios.

The Macintosh also established Apple’s preference for a controlled platform. Apple designed the hardware, operating system, core interface, and much of the user experience. This could limit choice, but it created a more consistent system than many competing PCs.

### Leadership conflict and Jobs’s departure

Apple recruited John Sculley from PepsiCo as chief executive in 1983. Jobs reportedly asked him whether he wanted to sell sugared water or change the world. The partnership initially gave Apple a more experienced executive leader.

Tension grew as Macintosh sales and organizational priorities changed. In 1985, Apple’s board supported Sculley in a dispute over control of the Macintosh division. Jobs lost day-to-day authority and resigned later that year.

Jobs founded NeXT after leaving Apple. NeXT built advanced workstations for higher education and business. The computers sold in limited volume, but NeXT developed software that would later become important to Apple. Tim Berners-Lee created the first World Wide Web server and browser on a NeXT computer.

Wozniak had stepped back from full-time work at Apple earlier in the decade. He remained associated with the company as a co-founder, but pursued other interests and projects.

### Apple in the late 1980s

During the late 1980s, Apple expanded the Macintosh line and built a strong business around education, publishing, and creative work. The company released desktop and portable Macs with increasingly capable processors and displays.

Apple also invested in software and media technologies. HyperCard, introduced in 1987, let people create linked stacks of information, buttons, and media. It influenced later ideas about hypertext and interactive authoring.

The company entered several new product categories. Some products succeeded, while others did not. Apple’s approach often involved introducing new interaction models before the market was fully ready.

The Macintosh operating system remained easy to use, but its technical foundations aged. As software grew more complex, Apple struggled to add modern features such as protected memory and preemptive multitasking without breaking compatibility.

### The 1990s: growth, competition, and complexity

The personal-computer market changed rapidly in the 1990s. Microsoft Windows gained share on IBM-compatible PCs. Intel processors became the dominant standard for many desktop computers. Microsoft’s software platform benefitted from broad hardware support and a large developer ecosystem.

Apple continued to sell Macs, but its market share declined. The company had higher prices than many PC manufacturers and faced pressure from lower-cost Windows machines. Apple also created a confusing number of product models, each with different features and names.

Apple experimented with licensing the Macintosh operating system to outside computer makers. Companies such as Power Computing produced Mac-compatible systems. The program increased the number of Mac systems, but it also risked shifting sales away from Apple’s own higher-margin hardware.

The company pursued several ambitious technologies. QuickTime advanced digital video on personal computers. The Newton MessagePad introduced handwriting recognition and a handheld computing concept. Newton drew attention but struggled with cost, performance, and recognition accuracy.

In 1993, Sculley left Apple. Michael Spindler became chief executive, followed by Gil Amelio in 1996. Each leader faced pressure to simplify the product line, improve the operating system, and restore profitability.

### The operating-system crisis

Apple’s aging System software created a strategic problem. The company worked on a successor called Copland, but the project became delayed and difficult to complete. Apple considered several paths to acquire or license a modern operating system.

In late 1996, Apple announced it would buy NeXT for approximately $429 million. The acquisition brought NeXTSTEP technology, experienced engineers, and Steve Jobs back to Apple as an advisor.

NeXT’s software used a Unix-based foundation and object-oriented development tools. It became the basis for Apple’s future operating systems. The decision allowed Apple to abandon the delayed Copland effort and begin a clearer technical transition.

Gil Amelio left Apple in 1997. Jobs became interim chief executive later that year. He would eventually drop the “interim” label, but it captured the uncertainty around Apple’s future at the time.

### Apple’s return under Steve Jobs

Jobs simplified Apple’s product strategy. A famous product grid divided the business into consumer and professional customers, with desktop and portable computers for each group. This reduced a sprawling catalog to a few clearer priorities.

The first major result was the iMac, introduced in 1998. The iMac used a colorful translucent enclosure, removed the floppy-disk drive, and adopted USB ports. It made a desktop computer look distinct from beige office hardware.

The iMac helped revive Apple’s brand. It also showed how product design could become a business advantage. Apple treated the computer as a complete object, combining industrial design, software, packaging, advertising, and retail presentation.

Apple introduced the Power Mac G3 and PowerBook G3 during this period. It also ended the Mac clone licensing program. Apple returned to selling a more tightly controlled hardware and software experience.

In 2001, Apple released Mac OS X. The system combined the familiar Mac interface with the Unix-based architecture derived from NeXT. Its Aqua interface introduced translucent visual elements, while the underlying Darwin system brought stability and modern networking foundations.

### Music, the iPod, and iTunes

Apple began reshaping digital music in 2001. The first iPod was a portable music player with a small hard drive and a scroll wheel. Its “1,000 songs in your pocket” message made its benefit easy to understand.

Early iPods worked primarily with Macs. That changed when Apple released a Windows-compatible iPod and iTunes software. The broader support helped the iPod become a mainstream consumer product.

The iTunes Store launched in 2003. It sold individual songs through a simple online storefront. Apple negotiated with record labels at a time when unauthorized file sharing had disrupted the music industry.

The iPod, iTunes, and iTunes Store formed an integrated system. Hardware, desktop software, online purchasing, and synchronization worked together. This model became a template for Apple’s later device-and-services ecosystem.

By the middle of the decade, the iPod dominated portable digital music. Apple introduced smaller iPod mini, nano, and shuffle models. The iPod also became a cultural symbol, supported by silhouettes advertising and white earbuds.

### Apple retail stores

Apple opened its first retail stores in 2001. Many observers questioned whether a computer company could operate successful stores. Jobs believed Apple needed direct control over how customers encountered its products.

The stores emphasized open product displays, trained staff, technical support through the Genius Bar, and locations in shopping centers and cities. Apple Stores became important sales channels and physical expressions of the company’s design standards.
